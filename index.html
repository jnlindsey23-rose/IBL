What Is Inquiry-Based Learning?

Definition: Inquiry-based learning is an approach in which learners explore meaningful questions, investigate ideas, use evidence, and develop explanations or solutions with appropriate teacher guidance.

Understanding the Inquiry Process

Although inquiry-based learning can look different depending on the question being explored, students generally move through several important stages. These stages help students investigate questions, make sense of information, and develop explanations. Five steps of the inquiry process:

  1. Ask a Question
  2. Investigate
  3. Examine Evidence
  4. Develop an Explanation
  5. Reflect and Revise

Inquiry is not always a perfectly linear process. Students may revisit questions, collect additional evidence, or revise their explanations.

The Teacher’s Role vs. The Learner’s Role

Who Does What During Inquiry?

Inquiry-based learning shifts much of the responsibility for exploring and constructing understanding to students. However, teachers remain essential in guiding the learning process.

The Teacher

  • Establishes learning goals.
  • Introduces meaningful questions.
  • Provides resources and materials.
  • Models and teaches when needed.
  • Asks guiding questions.
  • Provides feedback and support.

The Learner

  • Ask questions.
  • Explore ideas.
  • Collect information.
  • Examine evidence.
  • Develop explanations.
  • Shares and reflects on learning.

Student-centered does not mean teacher-absent. Effective inquiry requires both active learners and intentional teachers.

Why Does Inquiry-Based Learning Matter?

When students have opportunities to explore questions, investigate ideas, and develop explanations, they practice skills that extend beyond a single lesson.

1. Engagement and Curiosity

Students become active participants in learning by exploring questions that interest them and connecting learning to real-world experiences.

2. Critical Thinking

Students evaluate information, consider different possibilities, and use evidence to support their thinking.

3. Meaningful Understanding

Students connect new information to prior knowledge and develop explanations instead of only memorizing facts.

4. Communication and Collaboration

Students share ideas, listen to different perspectives, explain their reasoning, and learn from others.
